Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Fort Worth

The best way to avoid a surprise leak is simple maintenance. We recommend that homeowners flush the tank once a year (more often with hard water), have the anode rod checked every three to five years, test the relief valve annually to make sure it isn't stuck, and keep the thermostat around 120 degrees, which limits scalding risk and cuts down on scale. Tankless owners should plan on a descaling flush every year if their water is hard.

Take a look at the age of your water heater. The rating plate on the tank usually includes the month and year it was built, and that date tells you a lot. Most tank models reach 8 to 12 years, heat pump units 10 to 15, and well-maintained tankless units about 20. If your heater is beyond that range and also shows discolored water, popping sounds, or dampness under the tank, it's usually due for a planned replacement, which is almost always cheaper and less stressful than a rushed emergency install once the tank fails.

Small problems rarely stay small when they're ignored. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, affordable repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water begins corroding the tank itself, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to keep our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and prevents a flooded utility room.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

Most water heater failures we see in Fort Worth start with what's in the water. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, which produces the popping and rumbling you may hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling matters so much in hard-water areas.

Age matters just as much. Plenty of homes in the area still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and beyond about ten years, the anode rod is usually spent and corrosion starts working on the steel itself. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Well water can bring iron, sulfur, and grit into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a different rod can fix. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank on closed systems, a properly routed T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak would hit finished floors, and earthquake straps where local code requires them. All of these can be fixed, and most are much cheaper to address early. If something seems off, a quick diagnostic visit catches the small problem before it becomes an expensive one.
